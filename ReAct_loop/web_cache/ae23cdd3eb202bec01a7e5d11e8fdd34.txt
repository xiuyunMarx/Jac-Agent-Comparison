Our mission is to help bring creative projects to life. We are a\n[Public Benefit Corporation](/charter?ref=press).\n\nWorking on a Kickstarter story?  \nMembers of the press can email **press [at] kickstarter [dot] com** to contact\nus with a media inquiry.  \n[Visit our Help Center](https://help.kickstarter.com/) or [submit a\nquestion](https://help.kickstarter.com/en-us/articles/16797721-contact-us) to\nour support specialists if you're not a member of the press.\n\nThe basics\n\n  * __\n\nHow does Kickstarter work?\n\nThousands of creative projects are funding on Kickstarter at any given moment.\nEach project is independently created and crafted by the person or team behind\nit. The filmmakers, musicians, artists, and designers you see on Kickstarter\nhave complete control and responsibility over their projects. They spend weeks\nbuilding their project pages, shooting their videos, and brainstorming what\nrewards to offer backers. When they're ready, creators launch their project\nand share it with their community.\n\nEvery project creator sets their project's funding goal and deadline. If\npeople like the project, they can pledge money to make it happen. If the\nproject succeeds in reaching its funding goal, all backers' credit cards are\ncharged when time expires. If the project falls short, no one is charged.\nFunding on Kickstarter is all-or-nothing.\n\n  * __\n\nWhy do people back projects?\n\nA lot of backers are rallying around their friends' projects. Some are\nsupporting people they've long admired. Many are just inspired by a new idea.\nOthers are inspired by a project's rewards \u2014 a copy of what's being made, a\nlimited edition, or a custom experience related to the project.\n\nBacking a project is more than just giving someone money. It's supporting\ntheir dream to create something that they want to see exist in the world.\n\n  * __\n\nDo backers get ownership or equity in the projects they fund?\n\nNo. Project creators keep 100% ownership of their work. Kickstarter cannot be\nused to offer financial returns or equity, or to solicit loans.\n\nSome projects that are funded on Kickstarter may go on to make money, but\nbackers are supporting projects to help them come to life, not to financially\nprofit.\n\n  * __\n\nCan Kickstarter be used to fund anything?\n\nWe allow creative projects in the worlds of Art, Comics, Crafts, Dance,\nDesign, Fashion, Film & Video, Food, Games, Journalism, Music, Photography,\nPublishing, Technology, and Theater.\n\nEverything on Kickstarter must be a project. A project has a clear goal, like\nmaking an album, a book, or a work of art. A project will eventually be\ncompleted, and something will be produced by it.\n\nKickstarter does not allow projects to fundraise for charity or offer\nfinancial incentives. Check out [our rules](/rules?ref=press_basics) for\ndetails.\n\n  * __\n\nWho is responsible for completing a project as promised?\n\nIt's the project creator's responsibility to complete their project.\nKickstarter is not involved in the development of the projects themselves.\n\nKickstarter does not guarantee projects or investigate a creator's ability to\ncomplete their project. On Kickstarter, backers ultimately decide the validity\nand worthiness of a project by whether they decide to fund it.\n\n  * __\n\nHow does Kickstarter make money?\n\nIf a project is successfully funded, Kickstarter applies a 5% fee to the funds\ncollected. All pledges are processed securely by our third-party payments\npartner, Stripe. These payment processing fees work out to roughly 3-5%. View\nthe [fee breakdowns](/projects/fees?ref=press_basics).\n\nIf the project does not reach its funding goal, there are no fees.\n\nMore questions? [See even more\nFAQs](/help/faq/Kickstarter%20Basics?ref=press_basics)\n\nOur story,  \nas told by founder Perry Chen\n\n\u201cI was living in New Orleans in late 2001 and I wanted to bring a pair of DJs\ndown to play a show during the 2002 Jazz Fest. I found a great venue and\nreached out to their management, but in the end the show never happened--it\nwas just too much money...\u201d\n\nThe fact that the potential audience had no say in this decision stuck\nuncomfortably in my brain. I thought: \u201cWhat if people could go to a site and\npledge to buy tickets for a show? And if enough money was pledged they would\nbe charged and the show would happen. If not, it wouldn't.\u201d\n\nI loved the idea, but I was focused on making music, not starting an internet\ncompany. Yet slowly over the next few years I started to work on the idea more\nand more. In the spring of 2005 I moved back home to NYC, knowing it would be\nmuch more possible there.\n\nOnce back in New York, I started to try and tackle the next steps: Who could\nbuild the website? How much it would cost? Where could I get money? I talked\nto a bunch of folks and I learned a ton. I planned and planned.\n\nIn the fall of 2005, I met Yancey Strickler, and we became fast friends.\nYancey soon joined me in brainstorming. We bought a whiteboard. We had big\ndreams. I convinced some friends to give us a little bit of money. At some\npoint I made\n[this](https://s3.amazonaws.com/ksr/assets/000/002/455/4366d20f4880caaa63fdc01b4ec25765_large.jpg)\nrough design of the site. Clearly we needed more help.\n\nAbout a year later, I was introduced to Charles Adler, through an old friend.\nThe day after we were introduced, Charles came over to my apartment and he and\nI started working together almost every day. After months and months of\ncollaboration, we ended up with wireframes and specifications for the site.\n\nBut none of us could code. We had a few false starts hiring people to build\nthe site. There were months where not much happened. Charles moved to San\nFrancisco and took some part-time freelance work. Yancey was still at his day\njob. We had this money from our friends and not much was happening. It was\nemotionally draining.\n\nIn the summer of 2008 things finally started to move again. I was introduced\nto Andy Baio, who, though he was living in Portland, started to help us out.\nSoon after, Charles and Andy found a few developers -- including Lance Ivy all\nthe way in Walla Walla, Washington. We were a scattered team that lived\nthrough Skype and email (Charles had moved again, this time to Chicago), but\nwe were finally building -- even as the economy started to collapse.\n\nFinally, on April 28, 2009, we launched Kickstarter to the public. We told as\nmany friends as possible, and Andy announced it on his awesome blog Waxy.org.\nProjects trickled in. Yancey jumped into gear to handle all the new emails\nfrom people actually using (or wanting to use) Kickstarter. It was amazing!\nYou cannot imagine how excited we all were.\n\nThere are so many projects that defined the early days. Designing Obama, Robin\nWrites a Book, and Mysterious Letters were all landmarks. Filmmakers took\ntheir natural-born hustle and wrapped it around our template. People stepped\nup to support projects over and over again. It was thrilling. And then one day\nwe even had an office. In January 2010, nine months after we launched, we\nmoved into a tenement building in the Lower East Side of Manhattan along with\nCassie Marketos and Fred Benenson, our two new teammates.\n\nBut before all that, three weeks after Kickstarter launched, a young singer-\nsongwriter from Athens, GA, launched a project to fund her album, _[Allison\nWeiss was Right All Along](/projects/allisonweiss/help-make-the-new-allison-\nweiss-ep?ref=press_founder)_. Allison was using Kickstarter in the exact way\nwe had always dreamed. Her album was funded in one day. She did a Skype chat\nwith the backer that put her over her goal and posted it for all to see. This\nwas the moment Kickstarter was truly alive.\u201d\n\nPerry Chen\n\nRead the full story  Hide the full story  __\n\n![](https://i.kickstarter.com/20220922_Kickstarter_everette_0001-400x500-full-7460cfa.jpg?origin=static&sig=uQJMNDN2hu1H%2F22E%2Fcb38g1Tq3rzSNRnc9gKiryY%2BvM%3D)\n\nEverette Taylor is the CEO of Kickstarter, PBC.
