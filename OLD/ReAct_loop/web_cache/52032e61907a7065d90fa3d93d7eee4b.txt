Kaufman Prize for First Fiction\n2009\nHeartland Prize\n2024\nPulitzer Prize for Fiction\nWebsite\njayneannephillips\n.com\nJayne Anne Phillips\n(born July 19, 1952)\n[\n1\n]\nis an American novelist and short story writer who was born in the small town of\nBuckhannon, West Virginia\n. She was a professor of English at\nRutgers-Newark\nfrom 2005 to 2020 and helped establish the MFA program at\nRutgers University-Newark\n.\nEducation\n[\nedit\n]\nPhillips graduated from\nWest Virginia University\n, earning a\nB.A.\nin 1974, and later received an\nM.F.A.\nin fiction from the\nIowa Writers' Workshop\nat the\nUniversity of Iowa\n.\nTeaching\n[\nedit\n]\nPhillips has held teaching positions at several colleges and universities, including\nHarvard University\n,\nWilliams College\n, Brandeis University, and\nBoston University\n. She is currently Professor of English and founder/director of the\nRutgers University\u2013Newark\nMaster of Fine Arts in Creative Writing Program.\n[\n2\n]\nIn 2007,\nThe Atlantic\nmagazine named Phillips' MFA program at Rutgers\u2013Newark in its list of \"Five Up-and-Coming\" creative writing programs in the United States.\n[\n3\n]\nWriting career\n[\nedit\n]\nEarly career\n[\nedit\n]\nIn the mid-1970s, Phillips left West Virginia for California, embarking on a cross-country trip in the course of which she had numerous jobs, experiences, and encounters that would greatly influence her fiction, with its focus on lonely, lost souls and struggling survivors.\nIn 1976, Truck Press published her first short story collection\nSweethearts\n, for which Phillips earned a\nPushcart Prize\nand the Coordinating Council of Literary Magazines Fels Award.\nSweethearts\nwas followed in 1978 by a second small-press collection,\nCounting\n, issued by Vehicle Editions.\nCounting\nearned Phillips greater recognition and the St. Lawrence Award.\nHer next collection,\nBlack Tickets\n, published by Delacorte/\nSeymour Lawrence\nin 1979 when she was 26, was her first book of stories and brought her national attention as a talented and important writer.\nBlack Tickets\ncontained three types of stories: one-page fictions, inner soliloquies, and family dramas. These stories focused on the characters' loneliness, alienation, and unsuccessful search for happiness.\nBlack Tickets\nis mentioned in the 2006 lectures for the Modern Scholar series installment\nFrom Here to Infinity\n, by Professor\nMichael D. C. Drout\n, who refers to her style \u2014\u2014 he asserts it had a direct influence on\nWilliam Gibson\n's 1984\ncyberpunk\nnovel\nNeuromancer\n\u2014 as a \"headlong rush of story and description\".\n[\n4\n]\nCalled \"the unmistakable work of early genius\" by Tillie Olsen,\nBlack Tickets\nwas praised by\nRaymond Carver\n: \"These stories of America's disenfranchised \u2013 men and women light-years away from the American Dream \u2013 are quite unlike any in our literature\n... this book is a crooked beauty.\"\nBlack Tickets\nwas awarded the Sue Kaufman Prize for First Fiction by the\nAmerican Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters\n.\n[\ncitation needed\n]\nIn 1984, Phillips published her first novel,\nMachine Dreams\n, \"a remarkable novelistic debut and an enduring literary achievement,\" according to the\nNew York Times\n.\nMachine Dreams\nis a chronicle of the Hampson family from World War II to the Vietnam War. A\nNational Book Critics Circle Award\nFinalist in Fiction, it was named one of 12 Best Books of the Year cited by the\nNew York Times\n. It appeared on several Bestseller lists and was optioned as a film by actor\nJessica Lange\n, who wrote the screenplay.  Nobel Prize winner\nNadine Gordimer\nsaid that\nMachine Dreams\n\"reaches one's deepest emotions. No number of books read or films seen can deaden one to the intimate act of art by which this wonderful young writer has penetrated the definitive experience of her generation.\"\nFast Lanes\n, a 1987 collection of ten stories, all first-person narratives, was praised as work by a writer \"in love with the American language.\" One of the stories from\nFast Lanes\n, \"Rayme,\" had been published in 1984 in\nGranta'\ns\nDirty Realism\nissue.\nIn 1994, Phillips published her second novel,\nShelter,\na portrait of the loss of innocence at a West Virginia girls' camp in the summer of 1963. Called \"a rich, vivid novel of moral and psychological complexity destined to stand alongside works by Faulkner and other Southern masters\" (\nVanity Fair\n) and \"a defiant, frighteningly beautiful novel as disturbing as its setting,\nShelter\nfeels like Phillips' bid for immortality\" (\nHarper's Bazaar\n). Shelter was awarded an Academy Award in Literature by the American Academy and Institute of Arts and Letters.\n[\ncitation needed\n]\nLater career\n[\nedit\n]\nPhillips' next novel was\nMotherKind\n(2000), winner of the\nMassachusetts Book Award\n, a story of intergenerational love and struggles within a family facing many changes. It is praised as one of the best novels about mothers and infants and the mother/daughter bond.\nLark and Termite\n, her fourth novel, was published by\nKnopf\nin 2009 to positive reviews and was selected as one of five finalists for the\nNational Book Award\nin fiction.\n[\n5\n]\nLark and Termite\nwas also a Finalist for the National Book Critics Circle in Fiction;\nLark et Termite\n(French translation by Marc Amfreville) was a Finalist for the Prix de Medici Etrangers (Paris).\nQuiet Dell\n, Phillips' fifth novel, based on the true story of the 1931 murders of Chicago widow Asta Eicher and her three children in the hamlet of Quiet Dell, West Virginia, is a fictional portrayal of one of the nation's first sensationalized serial murders.\nQuiet Dell\ntakes as its protagonist nine-year-old Annabel Eicher (victim, with her family, of con man\nHarry Powers\n, who found his victims through Depression-era matrimonial agencies) and Emily Thornhill, a\nChicago Tribune\njournalist who commits herself to finding justice for the Eichers.  A Kirkus Review Fiction Pick of the Year and\nWall Street Journal\nBest Book of the Year,\nQuiet Dell\nwas called \"a story both splendid and irreparably sad\" by the\nChicago Tribune\n: \"As Phillips has proved throughout her decades of fiction writing, there is evil in the world but there are some who will stand in its way.\"\nQuiet Dell\nwas praised by the\nPhiladelphia Review of Books\n: \"It is the texture of the telling that elevates this recounting from true crime to the realm of literary eminence.\"\nPhillips' works have been translated and published in twelve foreign languages.\nAwards and honours\n[\nedit\n]\nShe is the recipient of a\nGuggenheim Fellowship\n, two\nNational Endowment for the Arts\nFellowships, a Bunting Fellowship from the Bunting Institute of\nRadcliffe College\n, a Rockefeller Foundation Bellagio Fellowship, and numerous other awards. In 2024, she was awarded the\nPulitzer Prize for Fiction\nfor her novel\nNight Watch\n.\n[\n6\n]\nSelected works\n[\nedit\n]\nShort fiction\n[\nedit\n]\nSweethearts\n(1976), prose vignettes\nCounting\n(1978), prose vignettes\nBlack Tickets\n(1979), short story collection\nHow Mickey Made It\n(1981), short story\nThe Secret Country\n(1982), short story\nFast Lanes\n(1987), short story collection\nNovels\n[\nedit\n]\nMachine Dreams\n(1984)\n[\n1\n]\nShelter\n(1994)\n[\n1\n]\nMotherKind\n(2000)\n[\n1\n]\nLark and Termite\n(2009)\n[\n7\n]\nQuiet Dell\n(2013)\n[\n8\n]\nNight Watch\n(2023)\n[\n9\n]\nReferences\n[\nedit\n]\n1\n2\n3\n4\n\"Phillips, Jayne Anne 1952\u2013 | Encyclopedia.com\"\n.\n\u2191\n\"Outstanding Faculty\n|\nRutgers University - Newark\"\n.
