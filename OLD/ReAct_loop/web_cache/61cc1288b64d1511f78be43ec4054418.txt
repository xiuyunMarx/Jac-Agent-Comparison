For other uses, see [IPO\n(disambiguation)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPO_\\(disambiguation\\) \"IPO\n\\(disambiguation\\)\").\n\nAn **initial public offering** (**IPO**) or **stock launch** is a [public\noffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_offering \"Public offering\") in\nwhich shares of a company are sold to [institutional\ninvestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutional_investor \"Institutional\ninvestor\")[1] and usually also to [retail\ninvestors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Retail_investor \"Retail\ninvestor\").[2] An IPO is typically\n[underwritten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwriting \"Underwriting\") by\none or more [investment\nbanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Investment_banking \"Investment banking\"),\nwho also arrange for the shares to be listed on one or more [stock\nexchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_exchange \"Stock exchange\").\nThrough this process, colloquially known as _**floating**_ or _**going\npublic**_ , a privately held company is transformed into a [public\ncompany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_company \"Public company\"). IPOs\ncan be used to raise new equity capital for companies, to\n[monetize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monetize \"Monetize\") the investments\nof private shareholders such as company founders or [private\nequity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Private_equity \"Private equity\")\ninvestors, and to enable easy trading of existing holdings or future capital\nraising by becoming publicly traded.\n\nWhen a company becomes publicly listed, the money paid by the investing public\nfor the newly issued shares goes directly to the company ([primary\noffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_market \"Primary market\")) as\nwell as to any early private investors who opt to sell all or a portion of\ntheir holdings ([secondary\nofferings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secondary_market \"Secondary market\"))\nas part of the larger IPO.[3] An IPO, therefore, allows a company to tap into\na wide pool of potential investors to provide itself with\n[capital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_\\(economics\\) \"Capital\n\\(economics\\)\") for future growth, repayment of\n[debt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt \"Debt\"), or [working\ncapital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_capital \"Working capital\").[3]\n\nAfter the IPO, shares are traded freely in the open market at what is known as\nthe free float. The market value of a company after an IPO is determined by\nthe share price multiplied by the total number of outstanding shares, commonly\nreferred to as market capitalization. [Stock\nexchanges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_exchange \"Stock exchange\")\nstipulate a minimum free float both in absolute terms (the total value as\ndetermined by the share price multiplied by the number of shares sold to the\npublic) and as a proportion of the total share capital (i.e., the number of\nshares sold to the public divided by the total shares outstanding). Although\nIPO offers many benefits, there are also significant costs involved, chiefly\nthose associated with the process such as banking and legal fees, and the\nongoing requirement to disclose important and sometimes sensitive information.\n\nDetails of the proposed offering are disclosed to potential purchasers in the\nform of a lengthy document known as a\n[prospectus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospectus_\\(finance\\) \"Prospectus\n\\(finance\\)\"). Most companies undertake an IPO with the assistance of an\ninvestment banking firm acting in the capacity of an underwriter. Underwriters\nprovide several services, including help with correctly assessing the value of\nshares (share price) and establishing a public market for shares (initial\nsale). Alternative methods such as the [Dutch\nauction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_auction \"Dutch auction\") have\nalso been explored and applied for several IPOs.\n\n## History\n\n[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Initial_public_offering&action=edit&section=1 \"Edit\nsection: History\")]\n\nThe earliest form of a company which issued _public shares_ was the case of\nthe _[publicani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Publican \"Publican\")_ during\nthe [Roman Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic \"Roman\nRepublic\"), although this claim is not shared by all modern scholars.[4] Like\nmodern joint-stock companies, the _publicani_ were legal bodies independent of\ntheir members whose ownership was divided into shares, or _parts_.[5] There is\nevidence that these shares were sold to public investors and traded in a type\nof [over-the-counter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-the-\ncounter_\\(finance\\) \"Over-the-counter \\(finance\\)\") market in the\n[Forum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Forum \"Roman Forum\"), near the\n[Temple of Castor and\nPollux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Temple_of_Castor_and_Pollux \"Temple of\nCastor and Pollux\"). The shares fluctuated in value, encouraging the activity\nof speculators, or _quaestors_. Mere evidence remains of the prices for which\n_parts_ were sold, the nature of initial public offerings, or a description of\nstock market behavior. _Publicani_ lost favor with the fall of the\n[Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Republic \"Roman Republic\") and\nthe rise of the [Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Empire \"Roman\nEmpire\").[6]\n\nIn the United States, the first IPO was the public offering of [Bank of North\nAmerica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_of_North_America \"Bank of North\nAmerica\") around 1783.[7]\n\n## Procedure\n\n[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Initial_public_offering&action=edit&section=2 \"Edit\nsection: Procedure\")]\n\nIPO procedures are governed by different laws in different countries.
