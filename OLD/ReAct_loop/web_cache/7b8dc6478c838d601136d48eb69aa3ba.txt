Chartered in 1755 by its first president\nBenjamin Franklin\n, who advocated for an educational institution that trained leaders in academia, commerce, and\npublic service\n, it is one of nine\ncolonial colleges\nin the country.\n[\n12\n]\nThe university has 4 undergraduate schools and 12 graduate and professional schools. Schools enrolling undergraduates include the College of Arts and Sciences, the\nSchool of Engineering and Applied Science\n, the\nWharton School\n, and the\nSchool of Nursing\n.\n[\n13\n]\nAmong its graduate schools are its\nlaw school\n, whose first professor,\nJames Wilson\n, helped write the\nU.S. Constitution\n, and its\nmedical school\n, the first in North America.\nIn fiscal year 2024, Penn reported\n$2.172\nbillion\nin research expenditures, ranking second among U.S. universities in the National Science Foundation's Higher Education Research and Development (HERD) survey.\n[\n14\n]\nAs of June 30, 2025, Penn's endowment was\n$24.808\nbillion\n.\n[\n15\n]\nThe main campus is in the\nUniversity City\nneighborhood of\nWest Philadelphia\n, centered around\nCollege Hall\n. Campus landmarks include\nHouston Hall\n, described as the first student union building in the United States,\n[\n16\n]\nand\nFranklin Field\n, which has hosted college football since 1895 and was expanded into a two-tier stadium in 1922.\n[\n17\n]\nThe university's athletics program, the\nPenn Quakers\n, fields varsity teams in 33 sports as a member of NCAA Division I's Ivy League conference.\nPenn alumni, trustees, and faculty include 8 signers of the\nDeclaration of Independence\n, 7 signers of the\nU.S. Constitution\n,\n[\n18\n]\n24 members of the\nContinental Congress\n, 3\npresidents of the United States\n,\n[\n19\n]\n[\ne\n]\n38 Nobel laureates, 9 foreign\nheads of state\n, 3\nUnited States Supreme Court\njustices, at least 4 Supreme Court justices of foreign nations, 32\nU.S. senators\n, 163 members of the\nU.S. House of Representatives\n, 19\nU.S. Cabinet secretaries\n, 46\ngovernors\n, 28\nstate supreme court\njustices, 36 living undergraduate billionaires, the only person to ever reach\ntrillionaire\nstatus,\n[\n20\n]\n6 recipients of the\nMedal of Honor\n,\n[\n21\n]\n[\n22\n]\nand more than 200 Olympic athletes, 43 of whom earned 81 medals (26 of them gold).\n[\n23\n]\nHistory\n[\nedit\n]\nMain article:\nHistory of the University of Pennsylvania\n18th century\n[\nedit\n]\nIn 1740, a group of Philadelphians organized to build a preaching hall for the traveling\nAnglican\nevangelist\nGeorge Whitefield\n. Designed and constructed by\nEdmund Woolley\n, it was the largest building in Philadelphia at the time and drew thousands of attendees.\n[\n24\n]\n:\n26\nIn the fall of 1749,\nBenjamin Franklin\ncirculated a pamphlet, \"Proposals Relating to the Education of Youth in Pensilvania,\" outlining his vision for a \"Public Academy of Philadelphia\".\n[\n25\n]\nOn June 16, 1755, the\nCollege of Philadelphia\nwas chartered, adding undergraduate instruction.\n[\n26\n]\nPenn identifies as the\nfourth-oldest institution of higher education in the United States\n, a claim contested by\nPrinceton\nand\nColumbia\nbecause the College of Philadelphia was not chartered until 1755 and its first board of trustees did not convene until 1749.\n[\nb\n]\nIn the 1750s, roughly 40 percent of Penn students needed lodging since they came from areas in the\nBritish North American colonies\nthat were too far to commute, or were international students.\n[\n27\n]\nBefore the completion of the first dormitory in 1765, out-of-town students were typically placed with faculty or in boarding houses.\n[\n28\n]\nJonathan and Philip Gayienquitioga, two brothers of the Mohawk Nation, were recruited by Franklin to attend the Academy of Philadelphia in 1755, making them the first Native Americans at Penn.\n[\n29\n]\nA 1765 admission ticket to \"A Course of Lectures\" given by\nDr. John Morgan\n, the founder and first professor of medicine at\nPenn's Medical School\nThe 1765 founding of the first medical school in America\n[\n30\n]\nmade Penn the first institution to offer both undergraduate and professional education.\nMoses Levy\n, the first Jewish student, enrolled in 1769.\n[\n31\n]\nThe first dormitory opened in 1765 under Penn professor\nEbenezer Kinnersley\n, who collaborated with Franklin on studies of electricity. Kinnersley served as steward of the dormitory, with disciplinary authority over the students.\n[\n32\n]\nMany students still sought housing elsewhere for greater personal freedom, and in 1775 the trustees voted to lease the dormitory to a private family who would board students at lower cost.\n[\n33\n]\nWhen the British were forced out of Philadelphia during the\nPhiladelphia campaign\nduring the\nAmerican Revolutionary War\n, College Hall, the college's only building at the time, served as the temporary meeting site of the\nSecond Continental Congress\nfrom July 7 to 20, 1778,\n[\n34\n]\nbriefly making Penn's campus one of the early\ncapitals of the United States\n.\n[\n35\n]\nIn 1779, distrusting Provost\nWilliam Smith\n's\nLoyalist\nsympathies, the revolutionary State Legislature created a new institution, renamed the\nUniversity of the State of Pennsylvania\nin 1785.\n[\n36\n]\nThis was the first American institution of higher learning to take the name \"University\".\n[\n37\n]\nSmith continued operating an attenuated version of the College of Philadelphia, and in 1791 the legislature issued a new charter merging the two institutions into the University of Pennsylvania, with twelve men from each institution serving on the new board of trustees.\n[\n36\n]\n19th century\n[\nedit\n]\nA\nc.\n1815\nillustration of the Ninth Street campus, including the\nmedical department\n(left) and the college building (right)\nIn 1802, the university moved to the unused\nPresidential Mansion\nat Ninth and\nMarket\nStreets, a building both\nGeorge Washington\nand\nJohn Adams\nhad declined to occupy when Philadelphia was the nation's capital.\n[\n26\n]\nAmong the faculty in 1807 was\nBenjamin Rush\n, a professor of chemistry, medical theory, and clinical practice who was also a signer of the\nUnited States Declaration of Independence\n, a member of the\nContinental Congress\n, and\nsurgeon general\nof the\nContinental Army\n.\n[\n38\n]\nClasses were held in the mansion until 1829, when it was demolished. Architect\nWilliam Strickland\ndesigned twin buildings on the same site, College Hall and Medical Hall (both 1829\u20131830), which formed the core of the Ninth Street Campus. In 1849, with the founding of Penn's Eta chapter of\nDelta Phi\n, Penn students began establishing residential fraternity houses.\n[\n39\n]\nBecause Penn had limited housing near campus and students came from across the country, many chose fraternity housing rather than university-owned residences. Early fraternities included Delta Phi,\nZeta Psi\n,\nPhi Kappa Sigma\n, and\nDelta Psi\n, all located within walking distance of the Ninth Street campus.\n[\n40\n]\nAfter more than a century in downtown Philadelphia, the campus moved across the\nSchuylkill River\nto property purchased from the\nBlockley Almshouse\nin West Philadelphia in 1872, in an area now known as\nUniversity City\n. The new campus and its associated fraternities centered on the intersection of Woodland Avenue, 36th Street, and\nLocust Street\n. By 1891, the university had at least 17 fraternities.\n[\n41\n]\nPenn opened the nation's first university teaching hospital in 1874; the\nWharton School\n, the world's first collegiate business school, in 1881; the first American student union building,\nHouston Hall\n, in 1896;\n[\n42\n]\nand the only\nschool of veterinary medicine\nin the United States to originate directly from a medical school, in 1884.\n[\n43\n]\nIn 1879, William Adger, James Brister, and\nNathan Francis Mossell\nbecame the first African Americans to enroll at Penn. Adger was the first to graduate from the college (1883),\n[\n44\n]\nwhile Brister, who graduated from the\nSchool of Dental Medicine\nin 1881, was the first to earn a degree at Penn.\n[\n45\n]\nLewis Baxter Moore\nbecame the first African American to earn a PhD at Penn in 1896; his doctorate was in classics.\n[\n46\n]\nThe first women enrolled at Penn were Gertrude Klein Pierce and Anna Lockhart Flanigen, both physicians, admitted in October 1876 as \"special students\" at the Towne Scientific School to study chemistry. In 1878 they received certificates of proficiency in chemistry and continued postgraduate studies in organic chemistry with professor (later provost)\nEdgar Fahs Smith\n.\n[\n47\n]\nIn 1880,\nMary Alice Bennett\nand Anna H. Johnson were the first women to enroll in a Penn degree-granting program; Bennett was the first woman to receive a degree from Penn, a PhD.\n[\n48\n]\n[\n49\n]\nA\nc.\n1933\npostcard showing the Upper Quad section of\nthe Quad Dormitories\nlooking north to Memorial Tower\nFrom its founding through the late 19th century, Penn largely lacked university-owned housing, and a significant portion of the undergraduate population commuted from the\nPhiladelphia metropolitan area\n.\n[\n50\n]\nThe medical school, with roughly half the students, was an exception, attracting a more geographically diverse population.\n[\n51\n]\nConstruction of the\nQuadrangle Dormitories\nbegan in 1895, accommodating Penn's growth in acreage, buildings, and student population.\n[\n52\n]\n20th century\n[\nedit\n]\nDuring the early 20th century, Penn worked to attract students from around the globe. In 1910, Penn's first director of publicity created a recruiting brochure translated into Spanish, with approximately 10,000 copies circulated throughout Latin America. That same year, the Penn-affiliated Cosmopolitan Club began an annual \"smoker\" that drew students from 40 nations.\n[\n49\n]\nIn 1911, because Philadelphia's segregation-era housing regulations made it difficult to house international students, the Christian Association at the University of Pennsylvania hired its first Foreign Mission Secretary, Reverend Alpheus Waldo Stevenson.\n[\n53\n]\nThe Christian Association purchased 3905 Spruce Street adjacent to campus, which officially opened on January 1, 1918, as the International Students' House.\n[\n54\n]\nBy 1921, Penn's enrollment had grown to 12,000 students, including 253 from at least fifty foreign countries and territories.\n[\n55\n]\nSadie Tanner Mossell Alexander\n, who earned a degree at\nPenn's School of Education\nin 1918 and a master's in economics in 1919, was awarded the Francis Sergeant Pepper fellowship and in 1921 became the first African-American woman in the United States to earn a PhD from an American university.\n[\n56\n]\nShe was also the first African-American woman admitted to and graduated from the\nUniversity of Pennsylvania Law School\n(1927), and the first to serve as an editor of the\nUniversity of Pennsylvania Law Review\n.\n[\n57\n]\nUntil 1930, Penn's chief academic and business officer was its Provost. In 1930, Penn's Board combined the two positions, creating the office of President, and elected\nThomas Sovereign Gates\n, a Philadelphia banker who had served as a director of the\nPennsylvania Railroad\nand the\nBaldwin Locomotive Company\n, and as chairman of Penn's endowment fund.\n[\n58\n]\nFrom 1930 to 1966, there were 54 documented\nRowbottom riots\n, a student tradition that included car smashing and panty raids. After 1966, five additional Rowbottoms occurred, the last in 1980.\n[\n59\n]\nBy 1931, first-year students were required to live in the quadrangle unless they received permission to live with family. Even so, the undergraduate schools continued to have a large commuting population well into the post-World War II period; as late as the 1940s, two-thirds of Penn's women students were commuters.\n[\n60\n]\nGeorge William McClelland\n, who received his bachelor's, master's and PhD all from Penn, served from 1944 to 1948 as Penn's second president. During his tenure, the\nENIAC\n, the world's first all-electronic digital computer, was completed at Penn in 1946.\n[\n61\n]\nFormer Minnesota governor and perennial presidential candidate\nHarold Stassen\nserved as Penn's third president from 1948 to 1953. The trustees chose Stassen in part for his reputation as a fundraiser, as Penn was in a financial crisis. Stassen helped raise funds, cut costs, focused resources on selected departments, continued McClelland's campus expansion, and reformed intercollegiate athletics to conform to the new Ivy League. He contested the NCAA's prohibition on televising football games by entering a $200,000 contract with ABC, but backed down after the NCAA threatened to expel Penn.\n[\n62\n]\nAfter\nWorld War II\n, a surge of students enrolling under the\nG.I. Bill\nprompted the university to launch a capital spending program to overhaul its campus, including student housing. By 1961, 79 percent of male undergraduates and 57 percent of female undergraduates lived on campus.\n[\n63\n]\nIn 1965, Penn students learned that the university was sponsoring research projects for the United States'\nchemical and biological weapons program\n.\n[\n64\n]\nStudents petitioned Penn's fourth president,\nGaylord Harnwell\n(1954\u20131971), to halt the program, calling it incompatible with an academic institution. Faculty voted on November 4, 1965, to re-examine the university agency responsible for the project.\n[\n64\n]\nThe first openly LGBTQ+ organization funded by Penn was formed in 1972 by\nKiyoshi Kuromiya\n, a Benjamin Franklin Scholar and Penn alumnus (College Class of 1966), who created the Gay Coffee Hour. The group met weekly on campus, was open to non-students, and served as an alternative to\ngay bars\nfor gay people of all ages.\n[\n65\n]\nVartan Gregorian\n, who joined the Penn faculty in 1972 and served as the 23rd provost from January 1979 to October 1980, was widely considered the likely successor to become Penn's sixth president. The trustees instead chose\nSheldon Hackney\n, who served as president from 1981 through 1993.\n[\n66\n]\nIn 1983, members of the\nAnimal Liberation Front\nbroke into the Head Injury Clinical Research Laboratory at the\nSchool of Medicine\nand stole research tapes. The footage was edited by\nPETA\ninto the film\nUnnecessary Fuss\n, and after media coverage and pressure from animal-rights activists, the project was shut down.\n[\n67\n]\nPenn drew national attention in 1993 for the\nwater buffalo incident\n, in which a student who told a group of mostly Black female students to \"shut up, you water buffalo\" was charged with violating the university's racial harassment policy.\n[\n68\n]\nDr.\nClaire M. Fagin\nserved as interim president from July 1, 1993, to June 30, 1994, becoming one of the first women to serve as president of an Ivy League university.\n[\n69\n]\nJudith Rodin\n, who served from 1994 to 2004, was the first permanent female president of an Ivy League university.\n[\n70\n]\nDuring her presidency, Penn's fundraising and endowment grew, the university launched a community renewal program, and admissions selectivity increased. Rodin's administration encouraged revitalization in\nUniversity City\nand\nWest Philadelphia\nthrough public safety initiatives,\nWharton School\nalliances for small businesses, the development of buildings and streetscapes oriented toward the surrounding community, and the establishment of a university-led partnership school, the\nSadie Tanner Mossell Alexander University of Pennsylvania Partnership School\n.\n[\n71\n]\n21st century\n[\nedit\n]\nIn 2004,\nAmy Gutmann\nsucceeded Judith Rodin as the\neighth president of the University of Pennsylvania\n, serving until 2022, the longest tenure of any Penn president.\n[\n72\n]\nAmy Gutmann, University of P"}
