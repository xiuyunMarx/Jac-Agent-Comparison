Airport\n(\nIATA\n:\nCVG\n,\nICAO\n:\nKCVG\n,\nFAA\nLID\n:\nCVG\n)\nis a public\ninternational airport\nlocated in\nBoone County, Kentucky\n, United States, near the community of\nHebron\n. It is the primary commercial airport for\nCincinnati, Ohio\nand the\ntri-state area\n. The airport's code, CVG, is derived from the nearest city at the time of the airport's opening,\nCovington, Kentucky\n. The airport covers an area of\n7,000 acres (10.9\nsq\nmi; 28.3\nkm\n2\n)\n.\n[\n5\n]\n[\n6\n]\nIt is included in the\nFederal Aviation Administration\n(FAA)\nNational Plan of Integrated Airport Systems\nfor 2023\u20132027, in which it is\ncategorized\nas a medium-hub primary commercial service facility.\n[\n7\n]\nCincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport offers\nnonstop\npassenger service to over 50 destinations in\nNorth America\nand\nEurope\n,\n[\n8\n]\nhandling numerous domestic and international cargo flights every day.\n[\n9\n]\nThe airport is a cargo global hub for\nAmazon Air\n,\nAtlas Air\n,\nABX Air\n,\nKalitta Air\n, and\nDHL Aviation\n. The airport is currently the\n5th busiest airport in the United States by cargo traffic\nand 12th largest in the world. CVG is the fastest-growing cargo airport in North America.\n[\n10\n]\n[\n11\n]\n[\n12\n]\nHistory\n[\nedit\n]\nBeginnings\n[\nedit\n]\nPresident\nFranklin D. Roosevelt\n's administration approved preliminary funds for site development of the Greater Cincinnati Airport on February 11, 1942. This was part of the\nUnited States Army Air Corps\nprogram to establish training facilities during\nWorld War II\n. At the time, air traffic in the area centered on\nLunken Airport\njust southeast of central Cincinnati.\n[\n13\n]\nLunken opened in 1926 in the Ohio River Valley; it frequently experienced fog, and the\n1937 flood\nsubmerged its runways and two-story terminal building.\n[\n14\n]\nFederal officials wanted an airfield site that would not be prone to flooding, but Cincinnati officials hoped to build Lunken into the region's main airport.\n[\n15\n]\nOfficials from\nBoone\n,\nKenton\n, and\nCampbell\ncounties in Kentucky took advantage of Cincinnati's short-sightedness and lobbied Congress to build an airfield there.\n[\n16\n]\nBoone County officials offered a suitable site on the provision that Kenton County paid the acquisition cost. In October 1942, Congress provided $2 million to build four runways.\n[\n13\n]\nAlthough the last of the land necessary to build the airport was not acquired until May 1943, site preparation had already begun.\n[\n17\n]\nBy mid March 1944, it had been named\nGreater Cincinnati Airport\n.\n[\n18\n]\nThe airport was to be \"formally presented\" to the City of Cincinnati in June.\n[\n19\n]\nThe field opened August 12, 1944, with the first\nB-17 bombers\nbeginning practice runs on August 15. As the tide of the war had already turned, the Air Corps only used the field until it was declared surplus in 1945.\n[\n13\n]\nHowever, this was not before the first regularly scheduled air freight shipment in the United States arrived in mid-September, signalling the future importance of the airport.\n[\n20\n]\nOn October 27, 1946, a small wooden terminal building opened and the airport prepared for commercial service.\nBoone County Airlines\nwas the first airline to provide scheduled service from the airport and had its headquarters at the airport.\n[\n13\n]\n[\n21\n]\n[\na\n]\nThe first commercial flight, an\nAmerican Airlines\nDC-3\nfrom Cleveland, landed on January 10, 1947, at 9:53\nam. A Delta Air Lines flight followed moments later.\n[\n23\n]\nThe April 1957 Official Airline Guide shows 97 weekday departures: 37 American, 26 Delta, 24 TWA, 8 Piedmont, and 2 Lake Central. As late as November 1959 the airport had four\n5,500\nft (1,700\nm)\nrunways at 45-degree angles, the north\u2013south runway eventually being extended into today's runway 18C/36C.\n[\ncitation needed\n]\nIn the 1950s Cincinnati city leaders began pushing for expansion of a site in\nBlue Ash\nto both compete with the Greater Cincinnati Airport and replace Lunken as the city's primary airport.\n[\n24\n]\nThe city purchased Hugh Watson Field in 1955, turning it into\nBlue Ash Airport\n.\n[\n25\n]\nThe city's Blue Ash plans were hampered by community opposition, three failed\nHamilton County\nbond measures,\n[\n26\n]\npolitical infighting,\n[\n27\n]\nand Cincinnati's decision not to participate in the federal airfield program.\n[\n28\n]\nJet age\n[\nedit\n]\nMain atrium\nOn December 16, 1960, the jet age arrived in Cincinnati when a Delta Air Lines\nConvair 880\nfrom Miami completed the first scheduled jet flight. The airport needed to expand and build more modern terminals and other facilities; the original Terminal A was expanded and renovated. The north\u2013south runway was extended from\n3,100 to 8,600\nft (940 to 2,620\nm)\n. In 1964, the board approved a $12 million bond to expand the south concourse of Terminal A by\n32,000\nsq\nft (3,000\nm\n2\n)\nand provide nine gates for\nTWA\n, American, and Delta.\n[\n13\n]\nA new east\u2013west runway crossing the longer north\u2013south runway was constructed in 1971 south of the older east\u2013west runway. A fire on June 15, 1975, significantly damaged the old terminal building and killed two firemen.\n[\n29\n]\nIn 1977, before the\nAirline Deregulation Act\nwas passed, CVG, like many small airports, anticipated the loss of numerous flights; creating the opportunity for Patrick Sowers, Robert Tranter, and David and Raymound Mueller to establish\nComair\nto fill the void. The airline began service to Akron/Canton, Cleveland, and Evansville. In 1981, Comair became a public company, added 30-seat turboprops to its fleet, and began to rapidly expand its destinations. In 1984, Comair became a\nDelta Connection\ncarrier with\nDelta's\nestablishment of a hub at CVG. That same year, Comair introduced its first international flights from Cincinnati to Toronto. In 1992, Comair moved into Concourse C, as Delta Air Lines gradually continued to acquire more of the airline's stock. In 1993, Comair was the launch customer for the\nCanadair Regional Jet\n, of which it would later operate the largest fleet in the world. By 1999, Comair was the largest regional airline in the country worth over $2 billion, transporting 6 million passengers yearly to 83 destinations on 101 aircraft. Later that year, Delta Air Lines acquired the remaining portion of Comair's stock, causing Comair to solely operate Delta Connection flights.\n[\n30\n]\nIn the meantime, the airport had been renamed\nGreater Cincinnati International Airport\nin November 1978.\n[\n31\n]\nIn 1988, two founders of Comair, Patrick Sowers and Robert Tranter launched a new scheduled airline from CVG named\nEnterprise Airlines\n, which served 16 cities at its peak. The airline spearheaded the regional jet revolution in a unique manner by operating 10-seat\nCessna Citation\nbusiness jets in scheduled services. The flights became popular with Cincinnati companies. The airline served destinations including Baltimore, Boston, Cedar Rapids, Columbus (OH), Green Bay, Greensboro, Greenville, Hartford, Memphis, Milwaukee, New York\u2013JFK, and Wilmington (NC).\n[\n32\n]\nThe airline also became the first international feed carrier by feeding the\nBritish Airways\nConcorde\nat JFK. In 1991, the airline ceased operations because of high fuel prices and the suspension of the British Airways contract after the first Gulf War.\n[\ncitation needed\n]\nThe facility's name changed to\nCincinnati/Northern Kentucky International Airport\nat the start of 1992.\n[\n33\n]\nDelta Air Lines hub\n[\nedit\n]\nDelta Air Lines\nBoeing 767-300ER\nheading to Paris in 2015\nConcourse C in 2016\nIn the mid-1980s, Delta opened a hub in Cincinnati. Delta's continued growth at CVG then prompted them to spend $550 million to build their own terminal facility in the 1990s.\n[\n34\n]\nThe new terminal, known then as Terminal 3, opened in 1994 and would largely replace Terminal D. Terminal 3 consisted of three airside concourses, with most of Terminal D's gate space being repurposed into Terminal 3's Concourse A while Concourses B and C were new construction. Concourses A and B were parallel concourses connected to Terminal 3's main building by an underground walkway which also included\na people mover\n(a similar layout to Delta's main hub at\nHartsfield\u2013Jackson Atlanta International Airport\n). Concourse C was only accessible by shuttle buses and was a ground-level facility for regional aircraft used by\nDelta Connection\n(operated by\nComair\n). After the opening of Terminal 3, the former Terminals B and C were renamed Terminals 1 and 2 respectively, which continued to house non-Delta airlines.\n[\n35\n]\nAircraft operations dramatically increased from around 300,000 to 500,000 yearly aircraft movements. In turn, passenger volumes doubled within a decade from 10 million to over 20 million. This expansion prompted the building of runway 18L/36R and the airport began making preparations to construct Concourse D while adding an expansion to Concourse A and B.\n[\n36\n]\nAt its peak, CVG became Delta's second-largest hub, handling over 600 flights daily in 2005.\n[\n37\n]\nIt was the fourth largest hub in the world for a single airline, based on departures, ranking only behind Atlanta, Chicago\u2013O'Hare, and Dallas/Fort Worth.\n[\n38\n]\nThe hub served everything from a 64-mile flight to Dayton, to a daily nonstop to Honolulu and Anchorage, to transatlantic destinations including Amsterdam, Brussels, Frankfurt, London, Manchester, Munich, Paris, Rome, and Z\u00fcrich.\n[\n39\n]\nAdditionally,\nAir France\noperated flights into CVG for several periods for over a decade before finally terminating the service in 2007.\n[\n40\n]\n[\n41\n]\nWhen Delta went into bankruptcy in September 2005, a large reduction at CVG eliminated most early-morning and night flights.\n[\n39\n]\nThese initial cuts caused additional routes to become unprofitable, causing the frequency of low-volume routes to be further cut from 2006 to 2007. Planning for the new east\u2013west runway stopped, along with all expansions to current terminals; Terminal 1 was closed due to lack of service.\n[\n37\n]\nIn 2008, Delta merged with\nNorthwest Airlines\nand cut flight capacity from the Cincinnati hub by 22 percent with an additional 17 percent reduction in 2009.\n[\n37\n]\nConcourse C, which had opened in 1994 at a cost of $50 million, was permanently closed in 2008 and demolished in 2016, since it was built for smaller regional jets which were going out of fashion, and it was not connected to the people mover system.\n[\n42\n]\nFurther reductions in early 2010 caused Delta to close Concourse A in Terminal 3 on May 1, consolidating all operations into Concourse B. This resulted in the layoff of more than 800 employees.\n[\n43\n]\nBy 2011, Delta was down to roughly 130 flights per day at CVG.\n[\n44\n]\nAfter several years of cuts to its older fleet, which were cited as being cut due to high costs associated with rising oil prices, Delta's wholly owned and CVG-based subsidiary,\nComair\n, ceased all operations in September 2012, ending over three decades of operations.\n[\n45\n]\nIn 2017, the hub was downgraded to a focus city, which was eliminated in 2021.\n[\n46\n]\nRecent history\n[\nedit\n]\nConcourse B ramp tower\nUntil 2015, CVG consistently ranked among the most expensive major airports in the United States.\n[\n47\n]\nDelta operated over 75% of flights at CVG, a fact often cited as a reason for relatively high domestic ticket prices.\n[\n48\n]\nAirline officials suggested that Delta was practicing\npredatory pricing\nto drive away discount airlines.\n[\n47\n]\n[\n49\n]\nFrom 1990 to 2003, ten discount airlines began service at CVG, but later pulled out,\n[\n50\n]\nincluding\nVanguard Airlines\n, which pulled out of CVG twice.\n[\n51\n]\nAfter\nDelta\ndownsized its hub operations, low cost carriers began operations and have been sustained at the airport ever since.\n[\n52\n]\n[\n53\n]\nTerminal 2 was closed in May 2012, and CVG re-opened and consolidated all non-Delta airlines to Concourse A in Terminal 3 at that time, which became the sole terminal.\n[\n54\n]\nRenovation and expansion of the ticketing/check-in area and Concourse A took place that year to accommodate the move.\n[\n55\n]\n[\n56\n]\nTerminals 1 and 2 were torn down in early 2017 to construct an overnight parking and deicing area.\n[\n57\n]\nBoth concourses, the customs facility, baggage claim, and ticketing areas were renovated in late 2017 to mid 2018 under a $4.5 million plan.\n[\n58\n]\n[\n59\n]\nIn 2021, the airport opened a new rental car and ground transportation center adjacent to the main terminal.\n[\n60\n]\nFacilities\n[\nedit\n]\nTerminal\n[\nedit\n]\nFurther information:\nCincinnati Airport People Mover\nUnderground walkway leading to Concourses A and B\nThe airport has one arrivals without pre-clearance are handled in Concourse B.\n[\n62\n]\nConcourse A\nhas 23 gates.\n[\n61\n]\nConcourse B\nhas 28 gates.\n[\n61\n]\nInterior view of Concourse B\nArt\n[\nedit\n]\nMain article:\nWinold Reiss industrial murals\nA\nMastodon\nfossil at Concourse B\nThe current airport is home to 5 of 16 large\nArt Deco\nmurals created for the train concourse building at\nCincinnati Union Terminal\nduring the station's construction in 1932. Mosaic murals depicting people at work in local Cincinnati workplaces were incorporated into the interior design of the railroad station by\nWinold Reiss\n, a German-born artist with a reputation in interior design. When the train concourse building was designated for demolition in 1972, a \"Save the Terminal Committee\" raised funds to remove and transport 14 of the murals in the concourse to new locations in the Airport. They were placed in Terminal 1, as well as Terminals 2 and 3, which were then being constructed as part of major airport expansion and renovation. When Terminals 1 and 2 were demolished, the murals in those areas were stored and the new Security Screening building was designed to accommodate the heavy weight of the murals with the eastern \"store front\" windows designed to be removable to permit the future installation of the murals. The murals were also featured in a scene in the film\nRain Man\nstarring\nDustin Hoffman\nand\nTom Cruise\n. In addition, a walkway to one of the terminals at CVG was featured in the scene in the film when Hoffman's character, Raymond,\nrefused to fly\non a plane. The nine murals located in the former Terminals 1 & 2 were relocated to the\nFirst Financial Center\nin downtown Cincinnati, while five murals remain in Terminal 3 to this day.\n[\n63\n]\nAdditionally, there are several pieces of\nCharley Harper\nartwork in the Concourse B food court.\n[\n64\n]\nCargo hubs\n[\nedit\n]\nA DHL\nBoeing 767-200\n(N784AX) taxiing at CVG\nIn 1984, DHL opened its CVG hub and began operations throughout the world. DHL has completed a $105 million expansion and employs approximately 2,500 at CVG. Because of this growth, CVG stood as the 4th busiest airport in North America based on cargo tonnage and 34th in the world at the time.\n[\n65\n]\nAmazon Air\nBoeing 767-300 cargo aircraft at CVG, its primary cargo hub\nOn May 28, 2015, DHL announced a $108M expansion to its current facility, which doubled the current cargo operations. The money was used to double the gate capacity for transferring cargo, an expansion to the sorting facility, and various technical improvements, which was completed in Autumn 2016. In addition, this has provided many more jobs for the Cincinnati area, and will dramatically increase the airport's operations.\n[\n66\n]\n[\n67\n]\nOn January 31, 2017,\nAmazon\nannounced that its new cargo airline,\nAmazon Air\nwould pick CVG as its main worldwide shipping hub, following an investment of $1.49B in the construction and expansion of a cargo facility on the airport grounds.\n[\n68\n]\nThe company used\nDHL\n's facilities prior to the construction of its new facility. The hub is Amazon's principal shipping hub and was constructed on\n1,129 acres (457\nha)\nof land at the airport with a\n3,000,000\nft\n2\n(280,000\nm\n2\n)\nsorting facility and parking positions for over 100 aircraft. On April 30, 2017, Amazon began operations at CVG with 75 Boeing 767-200ER/300ER aircraft based at the airport and planned to have 200 daily takeoffs and landings from its CVG hub to destinations across the U.S. and internationally.\n[\n69\n]\nThe hub could create up to 15,000 jobs in the Cincinnati/Northern Kentucky region.\n[\n70\n]\nBy early 2026, the Amazon Air primary hub reached its full phase-one operational capacity, managing over 200 daily flights and solidifying CVG's position as one of the top five cargo gateways in North America.\n[\n71\n]\nThe 3,000,000-square-foot facility now processes an estimated 50 million packages per month, significantly impacting the regional logistics infrastructure and workforce in Boone County.\n[\n72\n]\nGround transportation\n[\nedit\n]\nThe\nTANK\n2X bus provides direct service to Florence and downtown Cincinnati.\n[\n73\n]\nCVG is served by\nInterstate 275\nvia exit 4.\n[\ncitation needed\n]\nAirlines and destinations\n[\nedit\n]\nPassenger\n[\nedit\n]\nAirlines\nDestinations\nRefs\nAir Canada Express\nToronto\u2013Pearson\n[\n74\n]\nSeasonal:\nMontr\u00e9al\u2013Trudeau\n[\n75\n]\n[\n76\n]\nAlaska Airlines\nSeattle/Tacom"}
